# Audit log contract

The audit log is historical operational evidence of meaningful engine activity. It helps developers, operators, and auditors understand what the engine observed and what it persisted at a point in time. It is not a live state store.

## Reliability

- A persisted onboarding state change has a corresponding audit fact when the transaction succeeds. The audit fact and the state change are atomic where the engine applies them together: either both persist or neither does.
- Callback-applied onboarding state facts and outbox state-transition facts follow the same atomicity rule where applicable.
- Receipt, rejection, mapping, endpoint-resolution, and resend observations are best effort. They may be absent if audit writing fails, and such a failure must not change the underlying engine or webhook behavior.
- Connector failure-decision observations — a retry, a fallback taking over, a swallowed failure, or a propagated one — are best effort in the same sense: a failed write is logged and swallowed rather than turning a working fallback into a failed submit. They are written in their own transaction rather than the caller's, because the connector chain that produced the decision runs inside the onboarding's submit transaction, and a row written on that transaction's manager would be rolled back with a failed submit — losing precisely the rows an operator would go looking for. Each is stamped with its `occurred_at` and its id at the decision, not when its write reaches the database: the writes are scheduled rather than awaited, so several are in flight at once, and stamping at the decision is what makes a chain's rows read back in the order the engine decided them rather than the order those writes happened to win.
- The outbox remains the source of truth for current delivery state and endpoint data. Audit records describe history and may repeat outbound payload data; they do not guarantee the current outbox state.
- The `api.*` family and the newer `webhook.*` and `connector.*` actions described below are best effort on the same terms: each is written in its own transaction rather than the caller's, scheduled and swallowed rather than awaited or surfaced, bounded so a burst of failures cannot outrun the writes and exhaust the connection pool, and a write that itself fails is logged and dropped. In particular, `ApiFailureAuditFilter` never alters the response it observes — it always delegates to Nest's default exception handling first and only then, outside that path, schedules the audit write — so whether that write succeeds, fails, or is dropped for being over the bound changes nothing about what the caller receives.

## What a record means

Each record identifies the actor, time, resource, and action involved, together with safe contextual evidence. State-change records may include the relevant before-and-after onboarding state. Context should support investigation without turning the audit log into a copy of request or application data.

The `connector.*` family records one decision a connector's error handler returned when its adapter threw: `connector.failure_retried` when the same connector is called again, `connector.failure_fell_back` when its declared fallback takes over, `connector.failure_ignored` when the failure is swallowed and the chain proceeds, and `connector.failure_propagated` when the failure is rethrown — either because the handler said so, or because the engine could not honour the decision it returned — a `retry` past the declaration's own attempt cap, a `retry` past the slot's 20-second waiting budget, a `fallback` at the end of the declared chain, or a value outside the four contract actions, each named in the row's `override` field as `attempts_exhausted`, `retry_delay_budget_exhausted`, `fallback_unavailable` or `invalid_handler_action`. Their metadata names the connector that failed, the connector that took over and its 1-based `fallbackPosition` in the chain, the handler that decided, the attempt number, the `retryDelayMs` waited before a repeat, and the failure's HTTP status and error class name. It never carries the error's message, the connector payload, a credential or an endpoint URL, which keeps this family inside the data boundary below rather than relying on an exception string being harmless. These rows are filed against the onboarding resource without joining the lifecycle trail: they describe something that happened to the onboarding on the engine's own initiative, not a state it entered, so a reader reconstructing the journey through the lifecycle actions alone does not see them.

The `connector.*` family also covers outcomes that carry no error-handler decision at all, because there was never a decision to record: `connector.execution_unavailable` when the call into a connector's adapter fails before the error-handler chain even runs — before the `try` that would otherwise give the row a real `handler`, `attempt`, or `connectorOrder` to name — and `connector.result_rejected` / `connector.result_invalid_data` when an adapter refuses a submission without throwing at all, returning a declared rejection reason or a field-level data problem instead of a result the executor can accept. These are typed separately from `connector.failure_*` (`ConnectorOutcomeAuditMetadata`, not `ConnectorFailureAuditMetadata`) rather than folded into it with placeholder values, because `attempt`, `errorHandler` and `connectorOrder` have no honest value for a call that never reached the chain. `connector.execution_unavailable`'s metadata carries the thrown error's class name and never its message; `connector.result_rejected` carries the adapter's declared, value-free rejection reason; `connector.result_invalid_data` carries a value-free field-to-reason-code map, the same shape `InvalidStepDataException` uses elsewhere in this contract. All three are filed against the onboarding resource, the same as `connector.failure_*`.

The `webhook.*` family also covers failures the outbox pipeline reaches on its own initiative, outside any single inbound or outbound request: `webhook.outbound_lease_expired` when a delivery's lease has gone stale long enough that the retry worker dead-letters it — flipping it to a permanent `ERROR` with no further delivery scheduled — recorded once per abandoned row rather than as a single count, so each dead-letter has evidence pointing at it individually rather than only a log line naming how many rows changed; `webhook.outbound_poll_failed` and `webhook.outbound_dispatch_failed` when the retry worker's poll loop or its per-row dispatch fails; `webhook.outbound_queue_job_failed` when a queued delivery job itself fails or errors; and `webhook.outbound_finalization_noop` when a delivery attempt's outcome can no longer be written back — its claim was lost to another worker, or the row is already gone — so the attempt's result has nowhere left to land. `webhook.inbound_signature_skipped` records that an inbound callback was accepted with signature verification skipped for that connector; it must not itself start rejecting callbacks, since its purpose is to expose that verification was off for this event, not to enforce that it wasn't. `webhook.outbound_intent_failed` records that persisting or enqueueing an outbound notification's intent failed before any outbox row could carry it forward, and `webhook.outbound_notification_dropped` records that a notification was correctly deemed undeliverable and dropped without ever creating one.

The `api.*` family records every rejected or unhandled response on the `/v1` HTTP surface: `api.request_rejected` for a 4xx, `api.request_failed` for a 5xx. A single global exception filter, `ApiFailureAuditFilter`, writes it for every request the surface reaches, rather than relying on each route to record its own failure — which is also why it closes gaps that future error paths would otherwise reopen for free. A row files under `resource_type` `api_endpoint`, `resource_id` the matched route template (`POST /v1/onboardings/:id/documents`), never the domain object the request concerned: an `api.*` row describes a rejected _request_, and a 404 is frequently a request naming an id that never existed, so filing the row under that id would invent a resource that was never found. When the route carries an id, it survives instead in `metadata.routeParams`, keyed by the route's own parameter names. The actor is `operator`, with the caller's identity when the request carried verified admin claims, otherwise `anonymous`.

One event produces one row. Several throw sites already write a precise domain-specific row before throwing — the `onboarding.rejected_attempt` outcomes, the inbound callback rejections, `connector.failure_*` — and the filter must not turn that into a second, redundant `api.*` row. The exclusion is marked per throw site rather than per exception class, because a class alone does not say whether a domain row was actually written on that particular path: `OnboardingNotFoundException` is audited when thrown from `submit` or from the manual-review action, where a domain row is written first, but not when thrown from `GET /:id`, `GET /:id/state`, or `GET /:id/transitions`, where there is no domain row to duplicate. The consequence is deliberate, not incidental: a path whose domain write was skipped or itself failed is left unmarked, so the filter records it — which is how this change closes gaps such as a rejected submit attempt that produced no domain row of its own.

`auth.*` denials are a deliberate exception to the one-row rule: a guard denial writes **both** an `auth.*` row and an `api.request_rejected` row. The guard's write is fire-and-forget by design, so at the moment it throws the write has not merely gone unconfirmed — it has not been attempted. Marking it would suppress the filter's row on the strength of the marker alone, and a database failure would then leave an auth denial with no audit evidence at all, where before there was one row. Auth denials are the highest-value security evidence in this log, so a guaranteed duplicate is preferred to a possible zero. Expect two rows per denial, and read the `auth.*` row as the authoritative one.

### Entry type

Every row carries an `entry_type` alongside its `action`: `action` says what happened, `entry_type` says how it went. It is one of `success` (the thing happened as intended), `rejected` (a deliberate refusal — the system worked and the caller was told no), `failed` (something broke), or `observed` (neither — a fact worth recording, such as a delivery correctly dropped or a signature check configured off). `rejected` is kept apart from `failed` because a 4xx is not an incident and a 5xx is; folding them together would bury real failures under routine client mistakes. It lets "every error" be one indexable predicate rather than a list of action names each reader has to keep in step with this contract.

Each action declares the entry type it writes in the same place the action itself is declared — its family's table in `audit.types.ts`, one line per action — and the family's `AuditService` method passes that declaration to the insert. Because each family's action type is derived from its table's keys, an action cannot exist without a class, and a reader wanting to know how a row will be classed reads the line that declares the action rather than a separate mapping.

## Data boundaries

Inbound audit observations exclude raw request bodies, inbound payloads, headers and signatures, credentials, endpoint URLs or paths, response or error bodies, exception text, and ordinary inbound submitted or mapped data.

The `api.*` family's data boundary is an enumeration, not a "redact the obvious" pass: a row ever carries only `method`, `route`, `status`, `code`, `errorName`, `routeParams`, and `fieldErrors`. It never carries the exception's message, its stack, the request body, headers, cookies, or query values. Exception messages are the specific risk this excludes: third-party SDK errors (Sumsub, Twilio, SendGrid) routinely embed endpoint URLs and query strings that can carry API keys, which is why `errorName` — the exception's class name alone — is retained and its message is not. `fieldErrors` holds only reason codes and key or field names, never the values submitted or the values a validator would have accepted, and that is verified rather than assumed across every source that can populate it: `InvalidStepDataException`'s `errors` map, `ConnectorSettingsInvalidException`'s `issues` array, and class-validator's constraint keys, captured by a custom `exceptionFactory` rather than its rendered message array — the messages, unlike the constraint keys, can echo submitted or allowed values back (`@IsIn` lists them).

Callback-applied audit metadata is a deliberate sensitive-data retention exception: the implemented record may retain the submitted data and connector result that were applied while persisting the callback-driven onboarding change. This exception does not permit retaining inbound transport or security material, and it should be treated as sensitive.

Complete outbound payloads are intentionally retained when an outbound notification is constructed or stored. This is an explicit sensitive-data rule, not an accidental logging side effect; outbound payloads may contain client-controlled or business data and are not generally redacted.

## Operational expectations

Audit data is sensitive. Restrict access to the audit interface and to systems that store or display its responses. Avoid copying audit records or payloads into logs, traces, analytics, tickets, or other secondary stores. Apply deployment-governed retention, export, and deletion controls, and display narrower evidence instead of full payloads when possible.

## Non-guarantees

The audit log is not cryptographically tamper-evident. Best-effort observations are not guaranteed to exist. Audit history must not be used as the source of truth for current outbox state.
